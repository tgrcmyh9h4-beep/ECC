# The English Alphabet and Sounds

## The 26 letters
English has 26 letters. 5 are **vowels**: A, E, I, O, U. The other 21 are **consonants**. (Sometimes Y works like a vowel, as in "my" and "happy".)
| Letter | Name sounds like | Example word |
| A a | "ay" | apple, cake |
| B b | "bee" | ball, baby |
| C c | "see" | cat, city |
| D d | "dee" | dog, door |
| E e | "ee" | egg, tree |
| F f | "ef" | fish, phone (ph = f) |
| G g | "jee" | go, giraffe |
| H h | "aych" | hat, house |
| I i | "eye" | ink, ice |
| J j | "jay" | jam, job |
| K k | "kay" | king, kite |
| L l | "el" | lion, lamp |
| M m | "em" | man, milk |
| N n | "en" | nose, nine |
| O o | "oh" | orange, open |
| P p | "pee" | pen, pizza |
| Q q | "kyoo" | queen, quick |
| R r | "ar" | red, rain |
| S s | "es" | sun, rose (s = z) |
| T t | "tee" | tea, table |
| U u | "yoo" | umbrella, use |
| V v | "vee" | van, very |

| Z z | "zed" (UK) / "zee" (US) | zoo, zero |
> **Tip:** When you spell your name on the phone, say each letter clearly: "My name is Ravi: R – A – V – I."

## English spelling is not always the same as the sound
In many languages, you say a word exactly as you write it. In English, this is not always true. The same letters can have different sounds:
- "ough" in **though** (oh), **through** (oo), **tough** (uff), **thought** (aw)
- "ea" in **eat** (ee), **bread** (e), **great** (ay)
- "oo" in **food** (oo), **good** (u), **blood** (uh)
Do not worry. You will learn the sounds by listening and repeating. When you learn a new word, always check how to say it. Use an online dictionary and press the speaker button to hear it.

## Short and long vowel sounds
### Short vowels
| Vowel | Sound | Examples |
| a | /æ/ as in "cat" | hat, bag, man, apple, black |
| e | /e/ as in "bed" | pen, red, ten, egg, desk |
| i | /ɪ/ as in "sit" | big, fish, milk, ship, sit |
| o | /ɒ/ as in "hot" | dog, box, stop, hot, not |
| u | /ʌ/ as in "cup" | bus, sun, run, cut, but |
### Long vowels
A long vowel often "says its name". A silent "e" at the end of a word often makes the vowel long.
| Short | Long (with silent e) |
| cap | cape |
| hat | hate |
| pet | Pete |
| bit | bite |
| kit | kite |
| hop | hope |
| not | note |
| cut | cute |
| tub | tube |
### Important pairs: ship or sheep?
Many learners say "ship" and "sheep" the same way. They are different! "Ship" has a short /ɪ/. "Sheep" has a long /iː/ – make your mouth wide like a smile and hold the sound longer.
| Short /ɪ/ | Long /iː/ |
| ship | sheep |
| sit | seat |
| live | leave |
| fill | feel |
| hit | heat |
| bit | beat |
| chip | cheap |
| still | steal |
Practise saying each pair out loud. "I live here." / "I leave at six."
### Full /uː/ and short /ʊ/
| Short /ʊ/ | Long /uː/ |
| full | fool |
| pull | pool |
| look | Luke |
| good | food (different spelling, same long sound in food) |

## Difficult consonant sounds
### TH – two sounds
Put the tip of your tongue **between your teeth** and blow air.
- Voiceless /θ/ (no voice, only air): **think, thank, three, thing, mouth, birthday**
- Voiced /ð/ (with voice, it vibrates): **this, that, they, mother, brother, the**
! "I tink so." => "I think so."
! "Dis is my bruder." => "This is my brother."
### V and W
- **V**: top teeth touch your bottom lip. Voice on: **very, van, voice, love, give**
- **W**: make your lips round like a small circle, then open: **we, water, window, wine, what**
Say: "**V**ery **w**ell", "**W**e **v**isit **V**ienna every **w**inter."
### P and F
- **P**: close lips, then pop: **pan, pin, pool**
- **F**: top teeth on bottom lip, blow: **fan, fin, fool**
### L and R
- **L**: tongue touches the top of your mouth behind your teeth: **light, love, long**
- **R**: tongue does not touch anything; curl it back a little: **right, red, road**
Pairs: light/right, lead/read, long/wrong, glass/grass, fly/fry.
### S and SH
- **S**: like a snake: **see, sip, sort, sell**
- **SH**: lips forward, like telling someone to be quiet: **she, ship, short, shell**

| Silent letter | Examples |
| k (before n) | **k**now, **k**nife, **k**nee, **k**nock |
| w (before r) | **w**rite, **w**rong, **w**rist |
| b (after m) | clim**b**, com**b**, thum**b**, bom**b** |
| h | **h**our, **h**onest, w**h**at (often), g**h**ost |
| l | ta**l**k, wa**l**k, ha**l**f, cou**l**d, shou**l**d, wou**l**d |
| t | lis**t**en, cas**t**le, of**t**en (often both ways) |
| gh | nigh**t**, ligh**t**, daugh**t**er, though |
| p | **p**sychology, recei**p**t |

## The -ed ending: three sounds
The past tense ending **-ed** has three pronunciations:
| Sound | When | Examples |
| /t/ | after p, k, f, s, sh, ch (no voice) | helped, worked, laughed, missed, washed, watched |
| /d/ | after voiced sounds | played, called, lived, opened, cleaned |
| /ɪd/ (extra syllable) | after t or d | wanted, needed, started, decided |
! "work-ed" (two syllables) => "workt" (one syllable)
! "want" + "ed" said as "wantt" => "want-id" (two syllables)

## The -s ending: three sounds
| Sound | Examples |
| /s/ | cats, books, stops, laughs |
| /z/ | dogs, pens, plays, lives |
| /ɪz/ (extra syllable) | buses, watches, washes, boxes, pages |

## Word stress
In English words with two or more syllables, one syllable is **stronger** (louder and longer). This is called **stress**. Wrong stress can make you hard to understand.
| Word | Stress (CAPITAL = strong) |
| photograph | PHO-to-graph |
| photographer | pho-TO-gra-pher |
| banana | ba-NA-na |
| computer | com-PU-ter |
| hotel | ho-TEL |
| important | im-POR-tant |
| comfortable | COMF-ta-ble |
| vegetable | VEJ-ta-ble |
| interesting | IN-tres-ting |
| develop | de-VEL-op |
### Nouns and verbs
Some words are a noun **and** a verb. Often the noun has stress at the front, the verb at the end:
| Noun | Verb |
| a RE-cord | to re-CORD |
| a PRE-sent (gift) | to pre-SENT |
| an OB-ject | to ob-JECT |
| an IN-crease | to in-CREASE |
| an EX-port | to ex-PORT |

## Sentence stress and rhythm
In a sentence, we stress the **important words** (nouns, main verbs, adjectives, adverbs, negatives). Small words (a, the, to, of, is, are) are weak and fast.
- I **WANT** to **GO** to the **PARK**.
- She **BOUGHT** a **NEW** **CAR** last **WEEK**.
- We **DIDN'T** **SEE** him.
English has a "da-DUM da-DUM" rhythm. Listen to native speakers and copy their music, not only their words.
### Weak forms
Small words often become very short in natural speech:
| Written | Natural speech |
| to | /tə/ — "I want tə go." |
| for | /fə/ — "This is fə you." |
| and | /ən/ — "bread ən butter" |
| can | /kən/ — "I kən swim." (but "Yes, I **can**." is strong) |
| of | /əv/ — "a cup əv tea" |

## Connected speech
Native speakers join words together:
- "an apple" sounds like "a-napple"
- "turn off" sounds like "tur-noff"
- "want to" sounds like "wanna" (informal)
- "going to" sounds like "gonna" (informal)
- "got to" sounds like "gotta" (informal)
- "Did you" sounds like "Didja"
- "What do you" sounds like "Whaddaya"
> **Tip:** You do not need to speak like this. But you must **understand** it, because native speakers do it all the time.

## Intonation: the music of your voice
- **Falling** tone (voice goes down) for statements and Wh- questions: "I live in Delhi. ↘" "Where do you work? ↘"
- **Rising** tone (voice goes up) for yes/no questions: "Do you like coffee? ↗" "Are you ready? ↗"
- Lists: rise, rise, fall: "I bought apples ↗, bananas ↗, and oranges ↘."
